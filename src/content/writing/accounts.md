You download something small. A timer. A converter. A calculator. You open it, ready to use the one feature you came for, and it asks you to create an account first. Email, password, maybe a verification link, maybe "continue with Google." For a timer.

This is so common that it barely registers anymore, which is exactly why it's worth stopping on. A tool that runs entirely on your phone, that needs nothing from the internet to work, is refusing to start until it knows who you are. Worth asking: why does it need that?

## The fair version of why this exists

Sometimes it genuinely does. If an app syncs your data across your phone and your laptop, something has to know that both devices are yours — that's an account doing honest work. If it stores your stuff so you don't lose it when your phone dies, same thing. There are features that simply cannot exist without an identity behind them, and asking you to sign in for those is reasonable and I'd do it too.

There's also a softer fair reason. A maker who can email you can tell you about a bad bug, or a fix, or a new thing you might actually want. Used with restraint, that's a normal way to stay in touch with the people who use your work. I don't think wanting a way to reach your users is, by itself, sinister.

## Where it stops being for you

The trouble is that most tiny apps asking for an account don't have any of those reasons. The timer doesn't sync anything. The converter doesn't store anything. The account isn't there because the *feature* needs it. It's there because the *business* wants it.

An account turns an anonymous person into a known, contactable, trackable user. It gives the company an email to market to, an identity to attach behaviour to, a number — "registered users" — to show investors, and a switch it can use later to make the free thing less free. Often the sign-up wall exists purely so that a thing which never needed your identity can start collecting it anyway. The cost to you isn't dramatic on day one. It's that you've handed a stranger a permanent way to reach you and a file with your name on it, in exchange for a thirty-second task.

As with tracking and ads, this rarely comes from malice. It's the same pull: "users" is the metric, the metric is in the meeting, and "make people sign up" reliably moves it. The fair reason (some features need identity) becomes cover for the unfair default (collect identity regardless).

## What I do instead

My apps don't have accounts, and the reason is simple: the features don't need one.

A timer doesn't need to know who you are to count down. A converter doesn't need a login to change a file that's already on your phone. So there's nothing to sign up for, nothing to verify, no password to forget, and no email of yours sitting in a database somewhere waiting to be marketed to, leaked, or sold with the company.

I'll be honest about the limit this imposes, because it's real. Without an account I usually can't sync your settings between your devices, and I can't quietly back your data up to rescue you when a phone is lost. If a future app genuinely needs one of those, it'll ask you to sign in *for that specific thing*, I'll say plainly why, and it'll still do everything it can without it. The rule is: an account has to earn its place by making the feature work — never just to make you reachable.

Everything is open source, so "this app has no account and isn't collecting an identity for you" isn't something you have to take on faith. It's in the code, with my name next to it.
