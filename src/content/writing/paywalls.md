There's a specific move I want to talk about, because it's the one that bothers me most and the one I see everywhere.

You search for an app to do a single, clear job — remove a background, convert a file, split a PDF. You find one. It looks right. You download it, you bring in your file, you press the button — and the button is the paywall. The thing the app is *for*, the thing its whole store listing promised, is the thing you can't do without a subscription. Often the price isn't even shown until you're standing right in front of the locked door, file in hand.

## The fair version of why this exists

Let me defend the honest part first, because there is one, and I don't want to be cheap about it.

Charging money for software is completely legitimate. Building a good tool is real work, maintaining it is real work, and "pay the person who made the useful thing" is the *cleanest* arrangement there is — cleaner than ads, cleaner than harvesting data, cleaner than a sign-up wall. A paid app with an honest price is often the most respectful option on the menu. I have no quarrel with paid software. I have a quarrel with one particular way of selling it.

There's even a fair version of the free trial. Letting people experience the real thing before they pay is reasonable. "Try it, then decide" is a fine deal when it's actually offered.

## Where it stops being for you

The line is crossed when the paywall is built to extract a decision instead of inform one.

A trial says: here's the thing working, now choose. The pattern I'm describing does the opposite. It withholds the core function until you've spent your effort — found the app, learned it, imported your file, formed the intention — and then springs the price at the exact moment quitting feels like a waste. That's not pricing. That's pressure manufactured out of your own sunk effort. The listing implied the app does the job; the app does the job *of getting you to the paywall*.

You can usually feel the tells. The price hidden until the last screen. The "free" that turns out to mean "watch it almost work." The annual plan pre-selected and the cancel link nowhere near the buy button. None of that is the behaviour of someone confident their tool is worth the money. It's the behaviour of someone who has measured, precisely, the moment you're least able to say no — and built the app around that moment.

And, the same as everywhere else in this series: this mostly isn't villainy. It's the optimisation gradient. Conversion is the metric, the metric is in the meeting, and "show the wall later, when they're committed" reliably moves it. Each tweak is individually defensible. The sum is an app designed against the person using it.

## What I do instead

There's no locked core feature in my apps. The thing the app is for is the thing the app does, for free, the first time, with nothing held hostage. The whole app is the free app.

I'll be honest about the economics rather than pretend they don't exist. This works because the apps are deliberately small and run on your device, so they cost very little to keep alive. If an app ever has a real, ongoing cost — a service it truly depends on — it might cost money, and if it does I'll say so plainly, up front, before you've spent any effort, and the charge will be for that real cost, not a toll on the feature you came for. There's also a "buy me a coffee" link. It is an offer with no lock behind it. Pay it or don't; the app is exactly the same either way.

That choice costs me the most reliable money-making mechanism in the business, and I know it. I'd rather have your trust than your trapped conversion.

Everything is open source. "There is no hidden paywall and the core feature is genuinely free" is not a promise you have to believe — it's something you can open the code and check, with my name on it.
